Preterism (partial and full)

Preterism is a way of reading prophecy rather than a millennial position. Partial preterists hold that the Olivet Discourse's tribulation and much of Revelation (often 4-19) were fulfilled in the Jewish war and the destruction of Jerusalem (AD 66-70), with Nero as the beast. They base this on the time texts ("this generation," "the time is near," Rev 1:3). They still expect a future bodily return, resurrection, and judgment, and are usually postmillennial or amillennial. Full (hyper-) preterists say everything, including the Second Coming and resurrection, happened by AD 70. That is outside creedal orthodoxy.

Order of events

  1. Jesus predicts the temple's fall within "this generation" (Matt 24:2, 34)
  2. False christs, wars, famines, persecution (AD 30-66)
  3. Abomination of desolation: Jerusalem surrounded by armies (Luke 21:20)
  4. Great tribulation: the siege of Jerusalem, AD 66-70
  5. The "coming of the Son of Man" as a judgment-coming on Jerusalem (Dan 7:13 imagery)
  6. The beast = Nero and Rome (666 = Nero Caesar)
  7. Church age: Christ reigns and the gospel advances
  8. Partial preterists: future bodily return, resurrection, and last judgment (Acts 1:11; 1 Cor 15)
  9. New heavens and new earth

Who holds it

  • Luis de Alcázar (Jesuit) — Vestigatio arcani sensus in Apocalypsi (1614), an early systematic preterist commentary
  • Eusebius — Church History 3.5-8: treats the fall of Jerusalem as fulfilling Christ's predictions (quoting Josephus)
  • R. C. Sproul — The Last Days According to Jesus (1998): engages J. Stuart Russell's full preterism and argues for a partial-preterist reading of the Olivet Discourse and the time texts, while affirming a future return and resurrection
  • Kenneth L. Gentry Jr. — Before Jerusalem Fell (1989), which argues Revelation was written before AD 70; "A Preterist View of Revelation" in C. Marvin Pate, ed., Four Views on the Book of Revelation (1998)
  • Gary DeMar — Last Days Madness (1991)
  • Douglas Wilson — When the Man Comes Around (2019)
  • Wise Disciple (Nate) — YouTube video "Is This the End? Israel-Iran War & What Revelation Really Says": warns against assigning modern nations to Gog and Magog, and reads much of Matthew 24 as fulfilled in the first century (he cites D. A. Carson on Jewish views of "this age")
  • D. A. Carson — "Matthew," Expositor's Bible Commentary (1984; rev. 2010). Not a preterist, but takes the AD 70 dimension of Matthew 24 seriously. He reads the discourse as answering both the temple question and the end question
  • Full preterism (not orthodox) — J. Stuart Russell, The Parousia (1878); Max R. King, The Spirit of Prophecy (1971). Critiqued in Keith Mathison, ed., When Shall These Things Be? A Reformed Response to Hyper-Preterism (2004)

Strengths

  • Takes the time texts seriously ("this generation"; "the time is near"; "the things which must soon happen," Rev 1:1).
  • Revelation was addressed to seven real churches facing real persecution.
  • AD 70 was a world-shaking fulfillment that Jesus clearly foretold (Luke 19:41-44; 21:20-24).

Hard questions

  • Requires an early date for Revelation (before AD 70). Most scholars, following Irenaeus (Against Heresies 5.30.3), date it to about AD 95 under Domitian.
  • Some "cosmic" language (resurrection, the new creation, every eye seeing Him) does not fit AD 70.
  • Full preterism denies the future bodily resurrection, which the creeds confess and Paul treats as essential (1 Cor 15:12-19; 2 Tim 2:18).

Solomon's assessment

Partial preterism has helped me see how much of Matthew 24 was a real warning to the apostles' generation, and how Revelation spoke to the seven churches. I think AD 70 is a true but partial fulfillment that previews the end, and that Revelation's horizon reaches beyond it. Full preterism must be rejected: the resurrection of the body is not behind us.

Common ground

Whatever the view: Christ returns personally and visibly, the dead are raised bodily, all are judged, and God makes all things new (Apostles' and Nicene Creeds).
