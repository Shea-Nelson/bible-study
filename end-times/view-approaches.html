Four ways to read Revelation

Separate from the millennial question is how Revelation's visions relate to time. Preterist: fulfilled mostly in the first century. Historicist: a map of church history. Futurist: mostly still future. Idealist: timeless truths about the spiritual war. Most modern commentators blend these ("eclectic").

Order of events

  1. Preterist: Revelation 4-19 fulfilled by AD 70 or the fall of Rome
  2. Historicist: seals, trumpets, and bowls trace the church from Rome to the Reformation and on
  3. Futurist: chapters 4-22 describe the final years before and after the return
  4. Idealist: the visions picture recurring realities in every age
  5. Eclectic: first-century address + every-age pattern + future climax

Who holds it

  • Steve Gregg — Revelation: Four Views, A Parallel Commentary (1997; rev. 2013), which sets the four approaches side by side
  • C. Marvin Pate, ed. — Four Views on the Book of Revelation (1998): Gentry (preterist), Sam Hamstra Jr. (idealist), Pate (progressive dispensational), Robert Thomas (classical dispensational)
  • Richard Bauckham — The Theology of the Book of Revelation (1993); The Climax of Prophecy (1993)
  • G. K. Beale — NIGTC Revelation (1999), eclectic modified idealism with a future consummation
  • Grant Osborne — Revelation, BECNT (2002), eclectic with a futurist leaning
  • Robert Mounce — The Book of Revelation, NICNT (1977; rev. 1998)
  • Isaac Newton; E. B. Elliott — historicist classics (1733; 1844)
  • Francisco Ribera; Luis de Alcázar — Jesuit futurist (c. 1590) and preterist (1614) commentaries

Strengths

  • Preterist: honors the original audience. Historicist: sees God at work in history. Futurist: takes the climax seriously. Idealist: makes the book usable in every age.

Hard questions

  • Preterist: can shrink the final consummation. Historicist: its timelines keep needing revision and are Western-centered. Futurist: can make the book irrelevant to its first readers. Idealist: can lose the real future.

Solomon's assessment

I read Revelation eclectically, as Beale and Bauckham do. It was a letter to real first-century churches about Rome's beastly power. It shows patterns that recur in every age. And it ends in a still-future return, resurrection, judgment, and new creation. For a novelist, that is freeing: the beast's pattern can recur in a future AI-era empire without claiming you've decoded the prophecy.

Common ground

Whatever the view: Christ returns personally and visibly, the dead are raised bodily, all are judged, and God makes all things new (Apostles' and Nicene Creeds).
